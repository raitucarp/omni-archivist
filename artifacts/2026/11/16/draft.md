

The morning air inside the vaulted drafting cloister smelled of damp limestone, talc, and the dry, alkaline chill of high-altitude basin slate. One hundred eighty monolithic chalkboards rose in silent, upright succession along the perimeter walls, their surfaces scarred by generations of Diophantine reduction. Between them, the light hung pale and severe, filtering through lancet apertures that admitted no warmth from the Sun Valley basin outside.

Folasade Adebayo stood before the Board of Scrutiny with her calipers tucked firmly into her breast pocket. Behind her, rows of junior geometers in drab, unbleached linen sat on backless oak benches, arranged with the quietistic precision of the Religious Society of Friends. It was the seasonal convocation of Rejoicing in the Law, an occasion when the Chait Institute traditionally reaffirmed its arithmetic covenant, celebrating the eternal immobility of numbers. Yet around the perimeter, a dozen tenured fellows had chosen to lounge around the rosewood lecterns, watching her with the lazy, clubbish contempt of men who believed reality had already been solved during the last corporate proxy fight.

"The Littoral shelf does not negotiate with calculus, Brother Lindqvist," Folasade said, her voice striking the slate without echo.

Torstein Lindqvist did not reply at once. Draped in the unadorned wool of an elder censor, his tall, septuagenarian frame loomed over the center rostrum. With slow, circular passes of a damp felt eraser, he wiped a clean circle onto the slate before him. Triangular flakes of dry skin peeled along his jaw—a faint, premature desquamation that he ignored with ascetic pride. He waited until the moisture evaporated completely into the thin alpine air.

"You bring ordnance into a house of proof, Sister Adebayo," Lindqvist murmured, his frost-pale eyes unblinking. "You propose to monkey around with Diophantine point counting using combustion. As if the discrete grace of an elliptic curve could be hammered like pig iron."

"I propose to factorize what your continuous models call continuous wash," she countered, stepping forward until the chalk dust puffed beneath her boots. "The wave crests off Delaware Bay are not sine functions. They do not roll. They snap into roots of degree five. The water sheds its continuum like burned skin, and you sit here reading a monastic homily on humility while the sea turns into a lattice."

A stir ran through the benches. The room was not accustomed to such naked insolence. For nearly two centuries, continuous mechanics had held sway only as a vulgar, approximate tool for artillery officers studying ballistic trajectories at the Battle of Monmouth Court House or mining engineers measuring diesel runoff. Here in the high basin, one did not approximate. One calculated exact integer points—or one ceased to exist.

Lindqvist rested his palms upon the rail, his fingers stiffening into segmented lines. "The Institute's charter is explicit. Section nine, subsection three: no mechanical compression of rational points over the complex plane shall be permitted within the territorial mandate. The Selmer groups are to be contemplated, not assaulted. What you call arithmetic shedding is merely an unposed observational aberration. A sensor artifact born of cheap allyl-coated prisms."

"An artifact?" Folasade laughed, sharp and contumeliously loud, the sound slicing through the quietist hush. She felt the blood surge lustfully behind her temples, that rapacious mathematical hunger that had kept her awake through three weeks of sleepless reckoning.

From beneath her heavy canvas duster, she produced a brass specimen cylinder. She unlatched the wing-nuts and withdrew a small, velvet-lined tray. Upon it rested a sliver of shale encrusted with desiccated brine, harvested forty miles east of Cape May. It did not display the gradual curve of sediment. The salt and the silicate had joined along crisp, non-continuous facets, adopting the unmistakable serrated outline of a high-degree algebraic leaf shape.

"Observe the boundary invariants," Folasade said, setting the tray upon Lindqvist’s dry chalkboard ledge. "This is not mineral crystallization under thermal gradient. There was no drop in temperature. The water did not freeze; it factored itself. Nayeli Tlacopan performed histological sectioning on the trapped brown algae: every cell wall has discarded its lipid membrane to calculate its own surface area over integers. Continuous life cannot maintain its congener when the local coordinates reforge themselves. If you cross out my survey requisition today, the desquamation will reach the canal locks by midsummer."

Lindqvist leaned down. His pale brow furrowed, casting deep geometric trenches across his parchment skin. For a second, his ascetic reserve fractured. He reached out with an ivory stylus, touching the brittle ridge of the brine specimen. A microscopic snap resonated through the timber of the desk, sharp as a rifle cap.

"A localized strain," Lindqvist whispered, though his jaw muscles worked with sudden tension. "An epidermal anomaly. It will defervesce if left unprovoked. Nature abhors your mechanical violence. What you desire is not understanding, Adebayo. It is a carnal dominion. You wish to mate ballistic pressure with number theory."

"Because numbers are physical, Elder," she said, leaning in until she could smell the vinegar and camphor on his robes. "And unlike your council, they do not require committee consent to move."

A murmur of scandal broke across the assembly. Several elders stood, their woolen hems rustling against the floorboards. Lindqvist raised a single, trembling hand, and the chamber subsided into an anxious, suffocating silence.

"The Board will not grant you Institute bullion," the Chief Censor said, his voice flat, carved from grey limestone. "Nor will we permit the requisition of standardized dampeners from the Sun Valley vaults. You wish to headquarter your heresy on the Atlantic littoral? You will do so on a shoestring. The treasury holds an unliquidated parcel of ninety-four thousand Senegalese franc reserves from the Atlantic telegraph concession of seventy-four. You may draw against that paper and nothing else. Furthermore, I shall accompany your vessel as Chief Censor of Scrutiny. If your breech closer discharges so much as a single grain of propellant without my seal, your guild credentials are revoked eternally."

Folasade did not bow. She merely picked up her brass cylinder, sealed the latch with an aggressive snap of her thumb, and turned on her heel.

"Rejoice in the law, Sister Adebayo," Lindqvist called after her, his tone heavy with funeral irony.

"The law has teeth now, Brother," she answered without looking back.

Outside, the high-altitude sunlight struck the basin with an almost surgical glare. Folasade descended the massive granite steps of the Chait cloister, her oilskin coat catching the mountain gusts. The wind was dry, smelling faintly of pine resin and pulverized lime from the quarry works below. Far beneath her, where the railyards met the basin floor, narrow-gauge locomotives crawled along their rigid iron tracks, their steam plumes vanishing instantly into the arid blue.

She stopped at the foot of the balustrade, breathing hard. Her right forearm prickled with an acute, maddening itch. Pushing back her sleeve, she looked down. Along the radial edge of her wrist, the skin was white, dry, and tight, marked by a dozen tiny, polygonal scales. She scratched at them with her thumbnail; they did not bleed, but fell away as dry, translucent flakes, like ground enamel from an antique dish. The epidermal vesiculation had already begun. It was not an infection, nor was it the pseudosmallpox that had swept the lower barracks during the winter turnover. It was the space itself. The metric was tightening its mesh, refusing the slop of continuous growth.

She took a slow, deliberate breath, tasting the thin mountain air, and forced herself to straighten up.

Down at the rail siding, Faramarz Boroumand was leaning against the rusted fender of an ordnance lorry, his thumbs hooked under his heavy webbed harness. His salt-and-pepper beard was flecked with machine grease, and he was chewing rhythmically on a strip of bitter orange peel. When he saw her approach, he spat a tiny yellow pip onto the cinders.

"Did the monks give you the gold?" he asked, his voice low and raspy as a hand-saw.

"They gave us paper," Folasade replied, climbing into the passenger bench. "Defunct West African colonial scrip. Enough to buy five drums of low-grade kerosene, two sacks of lentils, and forty rounds of .45-caliber blank propellant for the chamber."

Faramarz let out a sour, rattling breath and swung himself into the cab. "A shoestring budget for a suicide cruise. That iron cutter isn't built for a hardened sea, Sade. You know that, don't you? Delaware Bay isn't water anymore. The pilots out of Lewes say the surface acts like cold asphalt. If we hit a high-degree ridge at eight knots, the hull will shear off at the rivets. We’ll be swimming in broken glass."

"We will not be swimming," she said, reaching into her pocket to pull out a greasy linen ledger. "Because the water will not displace. We need the breech closer mounted to the keel plate before tomorrow evening. If Lindqvist wants to watch us fail, he will have to keep his eyes skinned from the gunwale while his own skin peels off his collar."

Faramarz turned the heavy brass ignition key. The diesel engine coughed, shuddered against its motor mounts, and settled into an uneven, iron-throated thrum. "And Nayeli? She’s down at the docks already, boiling needles. She told me her phials of biological sedative won't hold the tissue together. Said something about colpoxerosis and cellular sizing. Said the local amoebas are refusing to divide because they can't find an integer solution for their own volume."

"Then they will remain undivided," Folasade said coldly, watching the granite towers of the Institute recede in the mud-spattered wing mirror. "Reality is shedding its excess muddiness. Why should an amoeba be permitted to be sloppy when the universe demands an exact denominator?"

"You talk like a machine with a grudge, Sade."

"I talk like an arithmetic entity, Faramarz. Drive."

The lorry lurched forward, rattling down the serpentine gravel switchbacks toward the lowland plains. Folasade looked out over the vast, shimmering floor of the continent, where the grey contours of the land stretched toward the Atlantic shelf. She could feel the itch in her wrist spreading, deep and rhythmic, tapping like a telegraph key against the bone. It was not terror she felt, but a fierce, triumphant clarity. Let the old men lounge around their monolithic slates, crossing out heresies with damp rags. Out there, where the salt water met the sky, the complex plane was tearing itself open to reveal the immutable, discrete scaffolding of the world—and she would be the first to drive a steel piston straight through its heart.

Past the low shoals of Cape Henlopen, the Atlantic ceased to roll. It did not break in surf or froth into foam, but seemed instead to climb an invisible staircase of shears and ledges, shucking off the soft curve of Newtonian drag. 

At the brass helm of the survey cutter *Calyx*, Faramarz Boroumand held the spoke handles with scarred, grease-blackened thumbs, feeling every shudder of the twin marine diesels travel straight up through his collarbone. The acoustic sounding stylus scratched frantically across the rotating drum of the bathymeter, its carbon stylus gouging not the expected sinuous valleys of Delaware Bay, but a series of rigid, stepwise serrations. 

"The bottom isn't rising," Faramarz shouted over the churn of the reduction gears. "The bottom is stepping. Look at the return—it’s throwing an echo like dressed ashlar!"

Behind him in the wheelhouse, Folasade Adebayo leaned over the chart table with her copper-rimmed goggles shoved up into her tight, caliper-pinned hair. Her fingers, powdered with chalk and stained with dried walnut ink, traced the ink needle’s spasmodic twitching. Beside her, Torstein Lindqvist stood as motionless as an uncarved headstone, draped in his coarse wool cloak, watching the sea with the sullen, clubbish quietism of an elder whose homilies had gone unheeded.

"It isn't ashlar, Faramarz," Folasade said, her voice taut, pitched with that rapacious mathematical appetite that made Faramarz's gut tighten. She pulled a brass vernier rule from her duster, measuring the intervals between the drum's ink spikes. "Degree seven. Look at the spacing of the nodes. It’s an elliptic profile projected across the salt wedge. The water isn't flowing down a gradient; it is satisfying an algebraic curve across the complex plane."

"The bathymeter is rattling its guts out because the transducer diaphragm is fouled with sediment," Lindqvist said quietly, his voice cold, dry, and parched as old linen. "You monkey around with mechanical sonar, Adebayo, and mistake an echo-chamber harmonic for an ontological conversion. The sea remains water. The Religious Society of Friends sailed these same waters three centuries ago without encountering your Diophantine ghosts."

"Three centuries ago, Brother Lindqvist, the metric had not begun to peel," Folasade replied, her lip curling contumeliously as she spun the chart dial. "You sit there with parchment skin, pretending the world is still smooth because the calculus of your youth offered you an administrative peace of mind. But the water is shedding its continuum. It is casting off the muddiness of integration."

Faramarz spat a bitter orange peel into the scupper can by the engine telegraph. "Argue theology in Sun Valley, you two. Out here, my keel draws twelve feet of draft, and that sounding tape says we have nine feet of clearance above what ought to be a sixty-foot channel. And that return isn't mud."

He throttled back to four knots. The diesel pitch dropped from an open roar to a labored, rhythmic panting. 

From the companionway hatch, Nayeli Tlacopan emerged onto the swaying deck, balancing a brass specimen bucket and a rack of bone-handled pipettes. Her dark hair was plastered to her forehead with brine spray, and the stiff lapels of her lab coat were spotted with yellow drops of allyl reagent. She set the carrier down upon the chart trestle with a sharp clatter that made Lindqvist flinch.

"Look at this before you accuse the echo-sounder of lying," Nayeli said.

Inside the glass jar she held aloft, three liters of seawater sat completely immobile. The cutter was rolling on a three-degree list, yet the surface of the brine remained dead flat, cocked at an acute twenty-degree angle to the horizon without spilling against the rim. Inside the liquid, small translucent lattices had formed—not the soft, hexagonal stars of sea ice, but rigid, serrated plates that mimicked a jagged leaf shape.

Faramarz tapped the glass with the blunt tip of his pocket knife. It didn't slush. It rang like leaded crystal.

"Temperature?" Folasade demanded, her eyes wide, leaning lustfully over the specimen as if it were a plate of roast meat set before a starving wolf.

"Fourteen degrees Celsius," Nayeli said flatly. "No thermal deficit. No latent heat release. I introduced five drops of allyl ether to test for lipid coagulation, and the organic congeners didn't dissolve—they organized. The diatoms inside have discarded their flagella. Their cell membranes are undergoing histological crystallization, calculating their perimeter against prime integer vertices. It’s not freezing, Sade. It’s factorizing."

Lindqvist stepped closer, his heavy woolen hem brushing Faramarz's knee. Slowly, the elder lifted his hand from his cloak. His cuffs slid back, revealing wrists where the epidermal desquamation had advanced in geometric patches. The skin was pale, dry, and divided into neat, triangular facets that shed fine, chalky dust whenever his tendons flexed. He stared into the jar, his frost-pale eyes narrowing with a dark, stubborn dread.

"A chemical adulteration," the Chief Censor whispered, though his knuckles whitened on his walking staff. "An industrial effluent from the Dover straits. Some foreign propellant residue drifting on the Labrador current."

"Look out the forward glass!" Faramarz roared, slamming both palms down onto the brass wheel.

Two cable-lengths off the port bow, the grey swells had ceased their rolling symmetry. A long, crested wave rose three fathoms high, but it did not curl. Its forward face had fractured into a terraced battlement of flat, planar surfaces, like a colossal staircase carved from dark anthracite. The sunlight striking it did not refract in watery glints; it glanced off mirror-flat polygons with the harsh, surgical gleam of polished enamel.

"Full astern!" Faramarz lunged for the telegraph, dragging the heavy bronze levers back through the detents. 

The engine reversed with an explosive rattle of exhaust valves, the hull shuddering as the bronze screws bit into the water, fighting the cutter's forward momentum. But the sea was no longer offering fluid resistance. It felt thick, gritty, unyielding—like trying to turn an auger through wet plaster.

"Hold the helm!" Folasade braced herself against the binnacle, her face flushed with an ecstatic, feral radiance. "Keep your eyes skinned, Faramarz! Watch the crest! It isn't random shear! Look at the vertices—three, five, seven! It's a rational surface! The Diophantine roots are closing right in front of us!"

"It’s going to take my bow off!" Faramarz hollered.

The *Calyx* did not rise to the swell.

With a sound like a locomotive derailing across gravel, the vessel’s steel-sheathed stem drove straight into the base of the faceted wave. The impact tore the spoke wheel from Faramarz’s grip, throwing him hard against the brass binnacle. Nayeli was thrown against the chart locker, her rack of sample phials shattering across the linoleum, while Lindqvist fell to his knees with a dry, wooden thud, his lips moving in silent, desperate prayer.

A sickening shriek of tearing metal groaned through the deck plates beneath Faramarz's boots. The bow had not cut through water; it had scraped against something that possessed the sheer, uncompromising density of solid basalt. The wave crest did not collapse over them in spray. It remained standing, rigid, towering above the forecastle, its polygonal facets vibrating with a high-pitched, metallic whine that set Faramarz’s teeth on fire.

Then, with the abruptness of a dry stick snapping in winter, the wave simply shattered. It did not splash. It split into millions of razor-edged rhomboids that rained down onto the iron deck with the dry, deafening roar of hail, skittering across the hatch covers before dissolving into thin, salt-crusted vapor that left the timber bleached and dry.

Silence fell over the wheelhouse, thick and ringing with acoustic static.

Faramarz scrambled up from the deck, his breath coming in ragged, wheezing gasps. His left shoulder burned where it had struck the binnacle casing, and his palms were raw, prickling with the telltale, sandpaper stiffness of epidermal vesiculation. Below decks, the bilge alarm was chattering like an angry insect.

He kicked open the lower hatch and listened. The dry, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of weeping rivet heads echoed up from the forepeak.

"Three frames buckled," Faramarz whispered, his voice trembling with an exhausted, murderous fury. "The stem post is bent two inches off true. We didn't hit an obstacle, Sade. We hit the water itself."

Folasade did not look down the hatch. She was kneeling on the linoleum, her bare fingers picking up one of the few surviving rhomboid fragments that had blown through the open bridge door. She turned it in the grey maritime light, watching the facets catch the sun. There was no melting. Her skin where she touched the polygon turned chalk-white, the pores pulling tight, forming tiny, microscopic squares that refused to bleed.

"The complex plane has dropped its continuous shroud," she said, her voice dropping to a reverent, chilling whisper. "The equations have seized the matter. It isn't a sensor artifact, Lindqvist. It isn't an unposed refraction. It is a genuine geometric transmigration. The space has cast off its flabby curves."

Lindqvist struggled to his feet, leaning heavily against the bulkhead. His face had aged a decade in the span of thirty seconds. The dry flakes along his jaw had spread down his neck, disappearing beneath the rough collar of his Quaker-cut coat. 

"Turn us about, Boroumand," Lindqvist said, his voice cracked and hollow. "Turn this vessel back toward the Delaware capes. We have documented the disturbance. We will seal the logs, take our findings to the Board of Scrutiny, and allow the Council of Elders to construct a sanctuary. This is not for human hands. To pursue this arithmetic with ballistics is an abomination against conscience."

"Turn back?" Folasade spun on him, her eyes burning with contempt. "Turn back with our hulls cracked and three jars of salt? You want to scuttle back to your one hundred eighty chalkboards and lounge around while the littoral shelf calcifies into a tomb? If we go back now, the elders will declare a quarantine, read a homily over our corpses, and burn the data to save their obsolete calculus!"

"And what would you do, girl?" Lindqvist flared, raising his staff, his tremor giving way to a terrible, righteous indignation. "Drive this tin can into the next lattice? Let the sea reforge our bones into integers? You saw what happened to the brine! Your own skin is shedding like parchment!"

"I would isolate the polynomial," she snarled, stepping into his face, unyielding. "I would chamber a propellant charge into the breech closer, compress the localized manifold, and find out whether the universe answers to degree seven or degree eleven! We don't run from an arithmetic truth because it scratches our paint!"

Faramarz stood between them, his hand resting heavy on the iron telegraph handle. His heart hammered in his ribs like a loose piston, and the sick taste of bile rose in his throat. He looked out through the salt-crusted wheelhouse windows. 

To the west lay the calm, continuous shallows of the bay, where the water still moved with the sluggish, muddy grace of the old world. If he turned now, the dying diesels could limp them back to the coal piers of Lewes. They could beach the cutter before the forepeak flooded, cash in the remnants of their Senegalese scrip for train passage back to the interior, and let the academics in Sun Valley strangle themselves in their own committee rooms.

To the east, the ocean stretched away in an impossible, terrifying vista. The horizon was no longer a smooth, level seam between water and sky. It was a jagged, stepped ridge of terraced polygons, rising and falling in discrete, integer increments, catching the light like a field of black glass. If they heaved to out here, drifting over this brittle abyss to take optical cores, another rational wave could rise beneath them without warning and snap the cutter’s keel like an unbaked biscuit.

"Faramarz," Nayeli said from the corner, her fingers carefully wrapping a linen strip around her own peeling palm. Her gaze was clinical, terrifyingly steady. "If we leave now, the transitional boundary will advance behind us. By tomorrow, the mouth of the bay will be locked in discrete geometry. If we don't sample the fracture face while the metric is still active, we will never know whether the transmigration is expanding or merely defervescing."

Faramarz closed his eyes for a single, agonizing second. He thought of his grandfather’s steam tables, the smooth logarithmic arcs that had guided three generations of iron ships across the grey Atlantic, now rendered as useless as astrology. The old world was dying, not with an explosion, but with a dry, arithmetic rattle.

"Faramarz," Folasade pressed, her hand closing on his forearm. Her fingers felt like cold porcelain rods. "Give me four hours of drift. Let us measure the roots."

He opened his eyes and looked at the telegraph.

"May God forgive me for listening to geometers," Faramarz muttered.

He pulled the telegraph lever back to neutral, then shoved it to dead stop. The engines died with a final, oily cough, leaving the cutter floating in a dreadful, unnatural stillness. 

"Heave to," he barked, turning to the hatch. "Nayeli, get your quartz slides and your carmine stain up to the boat deck. Folasade, unlimber the vernier rigs. If the hull starts groaning again, I'm dropping the anchor through the ice—or whatever the hell that water is—and we’re running for the beach in the dinghy. We find the polynomial, Sade. Then we get the hell out of here before our lungs turn into slate."

Beneath the iron ribs of the survey cutter *Calyx*, the sickbay smelled of carmine stain, wood alcohol, and the sharp, garlic-stench of volatile allyl vapor. 

Nayeli Tlacopan adjusted the brass sub-stage condenser of her monocular microscope, holding her breath to keep from fogging the ocular. Light from an unshaded carbon filament bulb reflected off the small zinc specimen dish, illuminating a cross-section of Faramarz’s dermal scrapings. Through the compound lens, the cellular architecture did not present the familiar, yielding mosaic of stratified squamous epithelium. There were no gentle curves, no soft lipid membranes sliding against intercellular fluid in elastic equilibrium. 

Instead, the keratinocytes had organized along rigid, planar boundaries, their borders meeting at precise rational intersections that mirrored the facets of an algebraic leaf shape. 

Every cell was sizing its own membrane, adjusting its volume to satisfy an integer polynomial over the complex plane. Continuous osmotic drift had ceased; the cytoplasm within had decoupled from normal enzymatic turnover. It was not necrosis. It was not the non-infectious pseudosmallpox that had plagued the junior scribes during the winter proxy fight at the Chait Institute, nor was it the sterile, dry distress of colpoxerosis she had once catalogued in the high alpine sanatoriums of the Sun Valley basin. It was an ontological mutiny.

"The cells are refusing to divide," Nayeli whispered, her dry lips clicking against her teeth.

Across the narrow laboratory planking, Folasade Adebayo sat upon an overturned powder keg, cleaning a set of steel feeler gauges with a rag soaked in nitrocellulose solvent. Chalk dust clung to the heavy cuffs of her coat, and her knuckles were raw, scored with dark cross-hatchings where the inertial kickback of the .45-caliber breech closer had bitten her flesh. Behind her, stacked in wooden crattings beneath the damp bulkhead, lay the expedition’s remaining dry stores—meager provisions secured on a shoestring of depreciated Senegalese franc notes that the Council of Elders had surrendered merely to rid themselves of her insolence.

"Division requires an intermediate infinitesimal," Folasade said without looking up, her voice ringing with the dry, metallic resonance of an iron rod dropped on slate. "Why should a living congener be permitted to loiter in the muddy approximations of calculus when the littoral shelf has already cast them aside? A cell divides across real numbers only because it is too cowardly to factorize."

"It is not cowardice, Folasade—it is systemic cellular failure." Nayeli straightened up from the eyepiece, rubbing the bridge of her nose. Her fingers felt stiff, oddly smooth, like polished bone. "Faramarz cannot close his left hand around the throttle lever without the skin cracking along hexagonal fault lines. Two of the deckhands are spitting chalky phlegm because their tracheal linings are shedding continuous mucus to reforge themselves into polygonal scales. The entire ship is undergoing arithmetic desquamation. If we do not bathe their exposed tissues in an allyl sedative and steam back into the smooth waters of the upper bay, their alveolar capillaries will lock before midnight."

Folasade set the feeler gauge down with a sharp, contumelious slap against the zinc counter. 

Rising to her feet, she stepped into the narrow pool of electric yellow light. The younger woman's eyes were feverish, dilated with an eroticized mathematical hunger that Nayeli found far more terrifying than the calcified wave crests scraping against the cutter's hull outside. For Folasade, the world had ceased to be an environment to inhabit; it had become an arithmetic variety waiting to be conquered, an obstinate polynomial that must be forced to yield its integer roots under extreme ballistic duress.

"Let their skin shed," Folasade murmured, leaning lustfully over the brass barrel of the microscope. Her breath smelled faintly of bitter orange peel and cordite. "Let the outer enamel peel away. What you call a pathology is merely the biological continuum collapsing under its own unposed falsehood. You want to administer an allyl sedative? You want to drug them back into the slop of continuous biology? Look at the rational points on that slide, Nayeli! See how cleanly the coordinates mate!"

Nayeli forced herself to look away from Folasade's predatory grin, directing her gaze back to the specimen tray. "Look at the edge of the slide yourself, Sister. Look at what your communion actually costs."

Folasade peered into the brass cylinder.

Under the glass slip, where the tissue met the drops of saturated carmine dye, the crimson stain had failed to diffuse. Instead of spreading in a natural, capillary bloom, the chemical had halted along an invisible, stepped perimeter. It formed sharp, triangular crescents, unable to penetrate the rigid lattice of the cell walls. Every single keratinocyte was computing its surface area via Diophantine roots, locking its perimeter into discrete integers that refused the solvent. The surrounding saline wash had begun to defervesce, releasing microscopic bursts of thermal static that left tiny, circular pits in the optical glass.

"Beautiful," Folasade breathed, her hand trembling against the focusing knob. "It has eliminated the transcendental remainder. It calculates without friction."

"It is calcifying them alive!" Nayeli’s voice rose, fracturing the austere silence that the guild had cultivated since the early days when the Religious Society of Friends had first established their arithmetic hegemony. "You preach to me like an elder reciting a homily from the high cloisters, but you are not looking at living beings. You are looking at a mortuary ledger. If the cellular volume cannot fluctuate continuously, the ion channels seize. The sodium pumps stall because they cannot find an integer solution for their transmembrane potential! You are sentencing this crew to an abattoir of exact numbers!"

Folasade pulled back from the ocular, her expression freezing into an icy mask of academic contempt. 

"Our forebears measured ballistic trajectories at the Battle of Monmouth Court House with continuous equations, and what did it buy them?" Folasade sneered, her voice dropping to an insolent hiss that filled the cramped room. "Two centuries of mechanical muddiness! Two centuries of engineering that treated reality as a rubber sheet because no one had the intellectual courage to demand discrete solutions! If Faramarz loses his hands to an algebraic curve, his sacrifice redeems fifty generations of physicists who allowed continuous illusions to hold sway over human thought!"

Before Nayeli could answer, a sudden, dry twitch seized her own right forearm. 

A bolt of icy heat surged through her carpal tunnel. Nayeli caught her breath, reflexively reaching with her left hand to peel back the stiff linen cuff of her lab coat. Beneath the fabric, across the delicate skin over the flexor tendons, a spray of pale, diamond-shaped papules had erupted. They were dry, hard, completely bloodless, and arranged in an unmistakable prime-number progression that ran from her wrist to the crook of her elbow.

Panicked, she reached for a phial of bone-handled dissecting pins and carmine reagent. 

With trembling precision, Nayeli dipped an ivory-handled pin into the crimson fluid and pressed the wet steel point directly against the tip of her index finger. The flesh did not yield with the soft resistance of living dermis. It met the steel with a dull, ceramic click. The skin did not puncture; instead, the surface shed a minute, triangular scale of dead keratin that fluttered down onto the zinc table like a fleck of dried whitewash. She rubbed her thumb across the wound. The fingertip was no longer whorled with loops and arches; it had faceted into tiny, geometric planes that felt as slick and cold as mica.

The stain would not take. The crimson droplet sat upon her cuticle like mercury, perfectly spherical, repelled by a biological surface that had rejected continuous chemical affinity.

"You see?" Folasade said softly, her voice laced with chilling tenderness as she caught Nayeli’s wrist in a grip of iron. "The space inside the *Calyx* is already straightening up. Your own blood is recognizing the true metric, Sister. It knows the truth even if your institutional conscience cowers behind medical manuals."

"Let go of me," Nayeli whispered, her throat constricting.

Folasade did not let go. Instead, her feral gaze drifted past Nayeli toward the reinforced locker where the emergency medical supplies were secured under brass clasps. Stenciled upon the wooden lid was the seal of the Institute’s medical dispensary, enclosing twenty amber-glass carboys of concentrated allyl precursors—the very compounds Nayeli required to synthesize protective emulsions for the crew's deteriorating lungs.

"Those carboys," Folasade said, her eyes narrowing as an immediate, predatory calculation resolved in her mind. "What is their volumetric concentration?"

"They are medical-grade precursors," Nayeli said, struggling against Folasade's grip, a sickening dread uncoiling in her belly. "They are intended for topical sedatives and lipid synthesis. Directly beneath the stem, level with the foredeck, lay the first broad terrace of the Rational Plane. Its surface was dark, smooth as honed porphyry, marked by fine, razor-sharp veins of white salt that delineated the roots of the local Selmer varieties. 

Folasade Adebayo did not hesitate.

Lifting her foot, she stepped over the gunwale and brought her boot down upon the stone-water shelf.

The contact produced a clear, ringing note that vibrated through the bones of her leg, harmonic and pure. Her weight did not displace the surface by a hair's breadth. The stone met her stride with absolute, unyielding precision, welcoming her into the new metric with the silent indifference of an eternal truth.

Behind her, the others followed, their faceted boots striking the rational surface in an unbroken, rhythmic measure.

Without looking back at the iron tomb of the *Calyx*, the cadre turned toward the east, advancing into the infinite, stepped silence of the discrete world.
