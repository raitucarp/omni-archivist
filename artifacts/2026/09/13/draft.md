

Deep Conduit Vault 09-Argun did not sleep, for sleep was an unallocated thermodynamic loss. Three hundred meters below the desiccated riverbed, the air hung at a sodden, sulfurous forty-one degrees Celsius, dense with caustic lime-dust that turned every exhalation into a slow calcification of the bronchial tree. Along the curved walls of the gallery ran the great arterial siphon: eight vertical yards of rivet-studded naval brass, insulated by asbestos webbing and sweating brackish moisture under a continuous vacuum stress of four thousand kilopascals.

Orlo Mallow wiped his sounder with the greasy hem of his tunic. The brass rod, calibrated to the twelfth part of an inch, registered only a hollow, vibrating metallic ringing when tapped against the casing. There was no hydraulic dampening. No hydraulic fullness. To the acoustic ear he had cultivated over twenty years of conduit overwork, the pipe was not conveying liquid; it was singing the dry, shrill song of a parched throat.

Inside the Argun basin, life was measured to the exact fluid ounce. Every municipal allotment, every greywater filtration recycle-run, and every ration coupon stamped for the subterranean tenements was anchored to the mathematical assumption that Arterial Pipe 4 drew a steady eighty cubic meters per second from the deep karstic sinkholes. If that flow remitted even by five percent, the automated governors in the upper redoubts would initiate an algorithmic ration freeze across District 9. Three hundred thousand laborers, packed into the stepped limestone warrens, would wake to sealed taps.

He checked his hand-held telemetry terminal. The phosphor screen flickered in the alkaline haze, its amber phosphorescence registering a catastrophic thirty-two percent volumetric deficit at the sub-aquifer intake. 

"Impossible," he muttered into the sweat-slick rubber of his rebreather.

Yet the needles did not lie. 

Upward through miles of cracked stone, the sovereign canal-city pursued its fragile, synthetic romance with survival. In the climate-controlled botanical domes miles above his head, the technocrats of the Siphon Syndicate sipped chilled French vermouth beneath blooming yellow heads of Craspedia, insulated from the dead world by synthetic air and artificial breeziness. Down here, at the lowest tier of the machine, human biology was merely an electronegative filter engine destined to masticate nutrient blocks, excrete recycled brine, and degrease valve housings until chronic arthritic swelling immobilized the knees.

Orlo adjusted his brass-rimmed goggles. His pupils, widened in acute mydriasis against the dim glow of bioluminescent vat algae, searched the shuddering catwalk ahead.

To leave the deficit unverified was unthinkable. To report it immediately without pinpointing the mechanical cause would summon the Syndicate's administrative security squads, who would seal the district sluices, declare an engineering emergency, and let the tenements dry out under armed curfew while the elites preserved their private reserves. His fragile sense of shame—that quiet, lingering ethical friction he had buried beneath two decades of bureaucratic compliance—refused to let him turn back.

He stepped onto the suspended catwalk.

Superheated bleed from an atmospheric heat-exchange stack vented abruptly from an overhead conduit, enveloping the gantry in a blinding, dry fog. The heat hit him like an open kiln. His skin prickled under his salt-crusted canvas suit, the ambient temperature spiking beyond the threshold of human endurance as the ventilation draft failed. He caught the iron guardrail, the metal scorching his palms through his insulated work gloves.

Then came the rupture.

Thirty paces ahead, a corroded gasket on an adjacent secondary greywater bypass gave way with a wet, concussive crack. A pressurized jet of alkaline slurry shot across the void, striking the opposite limestone buttress and atomizing into a caustic, milky spray. It showered down over the inspection gantry, coating the rusted iron ladder rungs in a slippery, corrosive lather. 

Orlo lunged forward, throwing his weight against the thermal gale. His boots slid on the chemical slime coating the gangway. Below him, the dark abyss dropped into the deep drainage sumps where swarms of bio-engineered Anopheles larvae were cultivated in stagnant troughs to digest biological waste. A fall meant dissolving into grey sludge before his body even reached the sediment beds.

Reaching the junction station, he gripped the frozen handwheel of the primary siphon inspection port. The metal vibrated with a terrifying, irregular frequency—not the smooth, deep purl of dense water under laminar flow, but a violent, rhythmic hammering that threatened to tear the bracket studs from the rock face.

With a grunt of strained tibial effort, he threw his mass into the surveyor's wrench, forcing the brass bleed valve open a single quarter-turn.

It did not spray water.

With a deafening shriek that tore through his ear protection, the valve sucked inward. The pressure differential was entirely inverted. The siphon was not pumping; it was gasping. It was pulling dry cavern air into the arterial line with the desperate, hollow force of an empty syringe.

Orlo dropped to his knees, his breath catching in his throat as the suction screamed through the brass fitting. He slammed the port shut with his mallet, cutting off the catastrophic whistling vacuum before it could implode the inspection glass.

His hand trembled against the valve wheel. 

This was no minor metering fault. This was no localized accumulation of rust or loose packing fiber that could be cleared with a high-pressure solvent flush. The primary siphon of the Argun Sub-Basin was drawing void because the subterranean aquifer itself was no longer yielding fluid to the intake bells. A deep structural stoppage—or an absolute, unpredicted plunge in the basal water table—had severed the intake entirely. The arterial life of the entire continental reach was collapsing from the bottom up.

Silence returned to the vault, broken only by the erratic, dying hiss of escaping steam.

Orlo leaned against the hot brass manifold, gasping through a filter mask that felt choked with powdered chalk. His eyes burned with caustic sweat, the tears stinging raw tracks down his hollow cheeks. A numbing, paralytic dread gripped his chest, accompanied by a cold, bitter vindication: the warnings whispered by the old scavengers in the lower sumps had been true all along. The Siphon Mandate had sucked the planet's deep stone dry, reaching an irreversible terminal velocity years ahead of the Syndicate’s manicured algorithmic projections.

Two choices lay before him, each carrying the certainty of blood.

If he tripped the manual audit horn mounted on the vault wall, the alarm would sound directly into the Directorate security barracks. Syndicate enforcers would descend upon District 9 within minutes to immobilise the population, freeze all secondary rations, and seize the conduits under martial decree. But in doing so, he would make himself an active accomplice to a bureaucratic lie that would starve hundreds of thousands without warning when the pressure finally zeroed out.

His finger hovered over the terminal's mechanical key switches. 

He could not stop the systemic rupture with an audit horn. Panic would solve nothing; it would only invite the Praetorians to slaughter the parched before the pipes ran dry. If the blockage was lithic—if the deep intake was choking on something physical within the deep karst—the official syndicate models were useless. He affixed his personal seal to the report and transmitted the falsified data packet north northwest toward the executive towers.

The console chimed, confirming the lie. The municipal indicators on the surface would remain green; the water-rations for the morning shift would be dispensed as scheduled; the Syndicate overseers would continue their leisurely routines, unaware that the hydraulic clock was ticking down its final, desperate hours.

Instead, he turned his back on the light, stepping off the main gantry into the dark, unmapped access crawlway that plunged downward toward the abandoned drainage substation where Kray's forbidden telemetry still slept in the salt.

Down here, far below the humid breath of the inhabited tenements, the atmospheric pressure sat in stagnant equilibrium, dense with the bitter aroma of sulfurous brine, decaying phenolic insulation, and the dry, powdery musk of pulverised limestone. Thick trunks of corroded naval brass conduits traversed the vaulted ceiling like petrified intestines, their flanged joints wept over by long whiskers of vitriol and zinc bloom.

`SYNDICATE RECLAMATION AUTHORITY - ARCHIVAL NODE 4-B`
`INSERT SURVEYOR BIOMETRIC CREDENTIAL`

Centered squarely at the intake mouth of Arterial Pipe 4, stretching across four square kilometers of subterranean karst, was a colossal, branching calcified mass: a petrified, metamorphous growth of crystallized calcite, barite, and heavy sulfates, choking the primary siphon bells like a vast, mineralized fibroid tumor.

Every gallon he had carefully metered, every leak he had dutifully brazed, had served only to grease the skids for systemic annihilation.

It fell upon Deep Conduit Vault 09 not as an absence of sound, but as an overwhelming, physical weight. For the first time in three centuries, the air did not vibrate with turbine drag. No acoustic shrieks purled through the brass casings; no clattering pumps sucked void from the deep karst; no administrative klaxons wailed their synthetic warnings across the limestone reach. The amber emergency beacons flickered out, leaving only the soft, greenish luminescence of wild sump algae clinging to the weeping stone.

Orlo collapsed onto his knees, his hands trembling violently against the cold deck. Beside him, Thorne sat back against the bulkhead, her linen tunic torn and soaked with black grease, staring with unblinking, hollow eyes into the humid gloom. Marais Chen knelt over Dr. Kray, gently easing the old hydrologist onto a bed of canvas rags.

The old man was breathing—shallow, wet gasps that rattled through his hollow chest—but on his cracked lips sat a tranquil, childlike smile. His life's monstrous creation had been shattered by his own hands, and the earth had not broken.

Slowly, inexorably, the ringing in Orlo’s ears remitted, giving way to an impossible sound.

From deep inside the severed throat of Arterial Pipe 4 came a slow, rhythmic trickle. It was not the angry, superheated hiss of pressurized siphonage, nor the violent surge of an over-torqued intake. It was the soft, low murmur of natural seepage—water descending through limestone strata under the gentle, unforced pull of planetary gravity.

"The pumps are gone," Marais whispered, their voice hushed, as if loud speech might shatter the fragile stillness of the vault. "The primary turbine banks... they are scrap metal. There is nothing left to draw the water up to the surface domes."

Thorne raised her head, looking at the silent conduits with a gaze devoid of her former technocratic arrogance. "Without the high-pressure grid, the Siphon Mandate is nullified. The botanical gardens... the upper-tier climate systems... they will dry to dust within a week. The artificial civilization is finished."

The silence pressed in, demanding an immediate, irrevocable accounting.

Two divergent paths stood before the survivors in the dark.

They could crawl up through the shattered shafts to the surface, gather the surviving engineers and slop-sellers, and scavenge parts from the industrial ruins to reconstruct the high-pressure pumps. With enough welded brass and brute mechanical force, they could force the Argun basin back into artificial siphonage, preserving the privileged enclaves and the familiar, authoritarian certainty of hydro-rationing. But to rebuild the syndicate regime was to invite the very monster they had just slain: forced suction would strip minerals from the deep karst once more, initiating another fatal calcification cascade that would inevitably crack the continental bedplates.

Or they could accept the absolute death of high-pressure siphonage.

They could refuse to rebuild the mechanical turbines, turn their backs on the manicured botanical luxuries of the surface domes, and begin unsealing the long-forgotten gravity-fed aquifers buried deep in the limestone karst. To choose this path was to step into profound vulnerability: without high-velocity pumps, human settlement would have to abandon the grand vertical cities, dispersing downward into the damp canyon floors to survive on the modest, low-yield communal trickles the earth could naturally replenish.

Orlo stood up, his boots splashing softly in the clear pool gathering at the base of the shattered conduit.

He reached down and scooped a handful of water. It was cool. It tasted not of chemical biocides or recycled greywater soap, but of ancient limestone, deep salt, and raw stone.

"We do not rebuild the pumps," Orlo said, his voice echoing steady and resolute through the quiet vault.

Thorne looked up at him, her fingers tracing the jagged tear in her sleeve. "You understand what that means, Mallow? The Directorate falls. There will be no rations, no administrative safety, no mechanical guarantee. We will be beggars living at the mercy of the seepage."

"We were never masters of the water, Thorne," Marais Chen said softly, rising from Kray’s side and standing beside the surveyor. "We were parasites strangling our own throat to fill your crystal glasses. The machine is dead. Let it rot in the salt."

Kray turned his head, his cloudy eyes gleaming in the bioluminescent algae light. "Sound... sound the deep gravity channels, Orlo," the old man breathed, pointing a withered, trembling finger toward the dark mouth of the cavern. "The ancient Argun basin... it still breathes beneath the slate. We must conclude... what the earth can give of its own will."

Orlo nodded. Unhooking the brass sounding rod from his tool belt, he stepped past the shattered casing of the arterial siphon and walked toward the dark, weeping bedrock.

The age of naval brass and violent pressure had ended in fire and shattered stone; the long, quiet labor of listening to the living pulse of the subterranean earth had finally begun.

The silence that settled into the shattered belly of Deep Conduit Vault 09-Argun did not resemble peace; it resembled the sudden, concussive hush of an operating theater where the patient’s thoracic cavity has been cracked wide under unshielded lamps. 

No steam whistled. No high-pressure bleeder valves vented their scalded flatulence against the weeping limestone. Across forty miles of dead riverbed, the cyclopean extraction turbines—which had groaned without intermission since the era of the first subterranean siphon mandates—lay dead, their naval brass runners fused to their housings or sheared into serrated shrapnel. A cool, foreign draft, tasting faintly of ancient dolomite and subterranean damp, crept along the floor of the gallery, licking at the charred cuffs of Orlo Mallow’s trousers.

Beside the buckled foundation of Arterial Pipe 4, Dr. Haddon Kray lay propped against an overturned tool chest, his fractured shoulder immobilized in an improvised sling torn from a surveyor’s measuring canvas. The old hydrologist’s breath was a thin, wheezing flutter, yet his cloudy eyes remained fixed on the cracked face of the master litho-telemetry terminal like an apostate awaiting judgment at the altar. 

"Plug the acoustic leads directly into the basal karst, Orlo," Kray whispered, his parched lips splitting as he formed the words. "Bypass the syndicate multiplexers. We must sound the deep stone before the residual heat boils the fissure dry."

Orlo knelt in the pooling grey slurry, his calloused thumbs working the brass thumbscrews of the portable sounding set. The hand-held transceiver, scarred by decades of alkaline corrosion, had survived the blast that pulverized the mineral tumor, but its phosphor display flickered with dying, amber instability. He took the dual-tipped lithic probe—two slender rods of cold-rolled titanium tipped with piezoelectric crystals—and drove them ten inches deep into the raw basalt fracture exposed when the emergency blast sluice had torn the casing away.

Behind him, High Commissioner Vespera Thorne and Guildmaster Marais Chen stood motionless in the shadows. Thorne had stripped away the torn remnants of her flaxen tunic, standing now in a soiled undersuit of vulcanized synthetic wool, her manicured fingers clenched until the tendons showed like taut cordage. Marais remained crouched on an iron girder, their scarred chin resting on the intake valve of their rebreather harness, watching the flickering screen with the unblinking, predatory skepticism of a scavenger who had never known a spring that did not stink of industrial grease.

Orlo threw the manual knife switch. 

A soft, electrical hum resonated through the receiver chassis, followed by an agonizing void.

Across the cathode screen, the volumetric line did not climb. It did not undulate. It traced a stark, horizontal flatline across the zero-datum axis, registering an absolute volumetric rate of zero point zero zero cubic meters per second. 

Not a drop. Not a seepage. 

"Nothing," Marais rasped from the gantry, their voice dropping like lead into the sulfurous dark. "The blast was too hot. It didn't just pulverize Kray’s mineral tumor; it blew the floor out of the sub-aquifer. The basin is dead. We cracked the casing to escape drowning, and we bought ourselves three days before every cistern from here to the polar reach sucks dust."

Kray’s head dropped back against the rusted iron plates, an expression of profound, crushing defeat carving itself into the hollows of his cheeks. "A myth," the old man breathed, staring up into the hanging stalactites of calcified brine. "My life's work... forty years of lithic calculations, forty years of tectonic alerts... all of it built upon an idealized romance of the subterranean cycle. I believed the Argun still possessed an autonomous hydrostatic head. I thought if we severed the high-pressure siphon, if we ceased this ravenous, biomechanical overwork of the deep earth, the water table would rebound through the karst. But there is no rebound. The syndicate bled it to the marrow decades before I ever drew my first pump blueprint."

Orlo did not look away from the terminal. His acoustic ear, trained through twenty years of conduit maintenance to distinguish between the dead silence of an empty pipe and the deceptive dampening of high-density rock, caught a faint, rhythmic anomaly in the line resistance.

"Wait," Orlo said, his voice flat, cutting through Kray’s mounting hysteria.

He leaned over the chassis, tapping his blackened fingernail against the primary galvanometer. The diagnostic routine was running an automated redundancy check, but the firmware was not reading raw geological flow; it was still cycling through the corrupted parameters of the Siphon Syndicate’s operational software. The baseline algorithms, coded sixty years ago to monitor sixty-thousand-horsepower centrifugal turbines, were configured to ignore any hydrostatic motion below two thousand kilopascals of hydraulic head. To the machine’s institutional eye, water did not exist unless it was traveling at sixty miles an hour through naval brass, driven by brutal thermodynamic extraction.

"The diagnostic bridge is blind, Doctor," Orlo said, reaching for his wire-strippers. "The console isn't measuring the earth. It is measuring the absence of the syndicate’s suction."

With two swift, deliberate cuts, Orlo severed the logic board from the terminal's pre-amplifiers. He stripped the braided copper wire with his teeth, spitting the bitter taste of tin and lead into the bilge, and spliced the piezoelectric crystals directly into the raw acoustic galvanometer circuit, bypassing every algorithmic filter designed to monitor industrial throughput. 

"Now," Orlo said softly. "Listen to the rock, not the machine."

He closed his eyes and pressed the surveyor’s brass acoustic horn against the chassis housing.

At first, there was only the cold, dead hiss of atmospheric static—the planetary electromagnetic background that had droned through the crust ever since the old wars salted the sky with radioactive smog. Then, beneath the hiss, rising through millions of tons of ancient limestone like the slow, rhythmic breathing of a creature buried alive in the deep strata, came an unmistakable cadence.

*Purl-clack. Purl-clack.*

It was not the deafening roar of Arterial Pipe 4 gorging itself on the basin. It was a gentle, continuous percolation: groundwater descending through vertical karst chimneys, gathering in gravel lenses, and welling through subterranean bedding planes under nothing more violent than the ancient, patient pull of gravity.

On the modified galvanometer, the needle began to oscillate—not in the frenzied, erratic swings of cavitation, but in a slow, pendular arc. Twelve liters per second. Fourteen liters per second. A steady, unforced percolation rising through the fractured bedrock, filling the lower cisterns with clear, living mineral seepage.

Kray pushed himself up onto his uninjured elbow, his pupils widening in acute, tear-blurred wonder. He reached out with a trembling, lime-dusted hand, his fingers hovering over the rhythmic sweep of the needle as if touching the pulse of a resurrected child. 

"It percolates," Kray wept, the tears carving jagged channels through the alkaline crust on his cheeks. "The deep Argun lives. Not as an industrial ocean... not as the transcontinental river the cartels promised to justify their siphons... but as a living karst. A finite, fragile lung."

The inquiry into the limits of subterranean siphonage had reached its absolute, undeniable conclusion. The planetary aquifers had never been infinite reserves waiting to be conquered by megalithic engineering, nor had they dried into an absolute void; rather, humanity had spent a century mistaking the violent speed of extraction for the natural capacity of the earth. The syndicate’s volumetric quotas—the elaborate administrative fictions that measured survival to the twelfth part of an ounce while consigning the lower tenements to water-borne blights and slow dehydration—had been nothing more than an extractive delusion that nearly split the northern hemisphere apart.

A tranquil, reverent solemnity filled the vault, thick as incense.

Through the fractured inspection scuttle, a faint draft of cool, moist air rose from the depths, carrying the long-forgotten scent of subterranean damp—untainted by sulfur, innocent of machine oil. For the first time in his life, Orlo felt the bitter tension in his chest remit, replaced by an acute, grounding humility. Humanity was not dying of an unquenchable planetary thirst; it was dying of its own hubristic refusal to live within the quiet cadence of the stone.

Thorne stepped forward, the amber glow of the terminal reflecting in the dark glass of her eyes. "Fourteen liters per second across this sector," she murmured, her voice stripped of its technocratic hauteur, leaving only the clinical habit of a lifetime spent calculating resources. "That is less than a tenth of what the upper domes consumed in an hour. It is enough to keep the basin’s population alive, but only if every luxury is pulverized. No botanical domes. No synthetic climate regulation. No high-pressure distribution to the surface towers."

Marais Chen looked at the former High Commissioner, then down at the severed telemetry cables. 

"Then we face the reckoning," Marais said, their voice low and jagged. "The municipal transceivers are still connected to the emergency repeaters on the surface. We have two choices before the battery banks die."

The slop-seller gestured toward the brass master console. 

"We can keep these numbers to ourselves," Marais continued, their eyes narrowing. "We can lock the telemetry in this vault, declare that the aquifers are fragile beyond measure, and use the knowledge to govern what comes next. A new council. An honest one, perhaps, but a council that holds the keys to the cisterns. If the surface tenements learn how little water is truly down here, panic will tear the city to pieces before sunset. The hunger marches will begin; the parched will storm the valves and waste the seepage in a day of riots."

Orlo looked down at Dr. Kray, then turned his gaze upon Thorne. 

"And the second option?" Thorne asked, her voice trembling slightly.

"We broadcast the unvarnished lithic telemetry across every municipal band," Orlo answered, his voice ringing firm and clean against the quiet stone. "We publish the raw metrics to the tenements, to the slop-sellers, to the surviving dome residents, to every hand-held receiver left in the reach. We tell them the truth: that the high-pressure siphon is dead forever, that the old world of lavish hydro-consumption will never return, and that survival now belongs only to those willing to leave the high towers, descend into the riverbed, and build a commons around the natural gravity outlets."

Silence fell again over the ruined pulpit.

To conceal the data was to re-enter the familiar, seductive circle of power—to become the very gatekeepers they had just risked their lives to overthrow, rationing truth by the ounce out of an institutional terror of human disorder. It was the birth of another Syndicate under a nobler name.

"We broadcast," Thorne said quietly. Her chin lifted, and for the first time since the crisis began, her face bore the dignity of true moral authority rather than borrowed administrative rank. "I spent twenty years lying to the people of this basin in the name of civil order, and it brought us to the edge of the abyss. If humanity cannot survive the truth of its own home, then no machine of ours can save it."

"Broadcast," Dr. Kray agreed, his breathing easing into a steady, peaceful rhythm as he leaned against the rock. "Let them hear the heartbeat of the Argun. Let them know what it costs to live."

Orlo stepped to the master communication terminal. He did not draft a bureaucratic dispatch; he did not append administrative codes or political appeals. With steady, grease-stained fingers, he patched the acoustic galvanometer output directly into the transcontinental municipal horn array.

Across the dark vaults of Conduit 09, down through the crowded limestone warrens of District 9, and upward into the silent, dying luxury of the surface domes, the voice of the Siphon Syndicate was replaced by a single, rhythmic acoustic transmission: the deep, ancient sound of living water, percolating through three hundred feet of limestone karst, unforced, unmetered by tyrants, and flowing freely toward the light.

Orlo shouldered his sounding rod, looked once more at the shattered monuments of naval brass, and turned toward the tunnel leading to the surface riverbed, ready to meet the people who would gather at the newborn springs.

Above the dead limestone riverbed of the Argun, the post-aquifer sky possessed the chalky, incandescent glare of an unlidded eye.

No clouds drifted across the scorched troposphere. Centuries of atomic fallout and persistent chemical smog had stripped the upper atmosphere of moisture-bearing layers, denying the northern hemisphere its ancestral snowfall and leaving only an unblinking furnace illuminated by the harsh constellations of high summer. At ground level, where the cracked mudflats of the former river valley sprawled toward an empty horizon, the heat rose in wavering, gelatinous sheets. It struck the body not as mere weather, but as a punitive physical weight, dense with alkaline dust and sulfurous grit.

Through the fractured blast doors of the surface pumping citadel poured the remnants of a shattered civilization.

They emerged in two ragged columns, divided by decades of mutual predation. From the lower drainage tiers stumbled the subterranean laborers and greywater scavengers: gaunt, bent-backed figures whose salt-crusted canvas overalls reeked of condensation sumps and recycled brine, their pale skin pitted with chemical burns and the dry, wasting lesions of Hansen’s disease. Once cushioned within the refrigerated botanical domes, the former overseers now drifted like sleepwalkers across the parched silt, their tailored synthetic silks and moth-eaten sable coats dragging through the dirt, their unshielded eyes squeezed shut in agonizing mydriasis against the unmediated sun.

Here, spanning sixty paces of fractured basalt, squatted the cyclopean headworks of the surface conduits. The high-pressure turbines that had once driven the transcontinental siphon were silent, their interior runners reduced to melted slag, yet the megalithic casing of naval brass remained intact. A hot, desiccating gale, scouring south-southeast from the dead Mongolian steppe, roared across the unsealed valve collars. The shallow, unpressurized trickles that had begun to pool in the concrete apron hissed against the sun-baked stone, evaporating into white steam before a single cup could be drawn. The world of naval brass conduits, hydraulic tyrants, and fluid ounces metered by bureaucratic terror had closed its ledger. Yet the desert remained unyielding.

As the noon heat intensified, the dilemma of their survival presented its hard, non-negotiable arithmetic.

If they dispersed along that newborn watercourse, the labor would be agonizing. They would have to live in tents and hand-carved stone shelters, fully exposed to the harsh desert storms and unshielded atmospheric radiation.
