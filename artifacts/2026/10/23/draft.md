From the cantilevered terraces of Station Epigraph-4, the Milky Way did not present itself as an untamed wilderness, but rather as an enormous, unfinished plate of stone. Below the station’s basal keel, the great barred spiral wheeled around its blinding nucleus like translucent washes of watercolor laid down by an unhurried hand. Two hundred billion suns merged into pale, coiled arms of dust and azure luminescence, drifting through the dark along tracks laid down in the primordial fog.

Sariha Bel-Habbash stood in the cold draft of the Epigraphic Resonance Cupola, rolling a smoothed pebble of raw quartz between her calloused thumb and forefinger. 

Around her, the station breathed with a dry, mechanical silence smelling faintly of heated quartz crystal and ionized ozone. It had been built centuries ago from blocks of terrestrial fieldstone hauled up the gravity well before the Great Dispersal, each block clamped to its neighbor by vacuum-bonded quartz collars. To the traditionalists of the Baseline Academies, that dry masonry was an altar to human fragility, an architectural pledge of humble transience. To Sariha, it was simply ballast—a stubborn, heavy refusal to drift.

She looked down through the dark filtering prism of her optical hood. Long ago, archaic baselines—the sprint of an early gasoline automobile, the petty civil counts of the Julian calendar, the fragile brick walls of ancient Aleppo—had measured the boundaries of human care. Now, deep-time archivists measured existence by the kinematics of the outer halo, where ancient dwarf galaxies unraveled over hundreds of megayears into long, ghostly ribbons of fossil stars.

Her terminal console flared with six hundred foot-lamberts of polarimetric radiance. The display charted the Ophiuchus stream, where a skinny tendril of metal-poor suns was threading the needle of the galactic corona. 

Sariha’s task this cycle was precise, meticulous, and entirely heretical. She was hunting for an uncorrupted dwarf spheroidal core caught within the halo’s Yazoo-type flows—those curious stellar currents that snaked along the outer magnetic ridges, paralleling the galactic disk for kiloparsecs before finding a cleft through the dust. If she could isolate a dense, coherent core nestled inside the stream’s magnetic zig-zag fold, the Epigraphic Faction could install resonance thrusters upon its nickel-iron spine. They could channel its descent down the gravitational ski slope toward the inner Lindblad resonances, burning an immutable kinematic signature across the disk for keeps.

Observation without inscription was a cowardice she could no longer stomach.

She leaned over the cold-stage X-ray diffraction console, keying the primary vacuum arrays to probe the refractory dust grains entrained in the stream’s leading edge. "Focusing on secondary cluster Ophiuchus-Delta," she murmured into the collar of her pressurized caftan. "Calibrating electron beam optics for high-resolution lattice capture."

Across her displays, crystalline interference patterns blossomed into concentric rings. She sought the exact chemical spiciness of the stream, counting heavy-element isotopes down to a fractional Avogadro number to confirm whether the cluster possessed the structural integrity for orbital manipulation. 

Instead, the sensor return stuttered.

The neutral hydrogen, compressed by the halo's sweeping magnetic folds, behaved like a colloid undergoing violent synaeresis, squeezing out its trapped fields in sharp, localized ruptures. "Sensor lock failing," Sariha muttered, her knuckles whitening against the console plinth. "Vector tracking is dropping resolution across the outer quadrant."

She did not panic. She reached for the operational console, administering a digital papaverine analogue to loosen the computational spasm, smoothing the algorithmic pipeline and refusing to round down the minute kinematic deviations.

A localized baryonic collapse inside the gas stream sheared across the station’s forward line of sight. It was not an explosion, but a sudden, silent redistribution of mass—a vast pocket of cold molecular hydrogen collapsing into gravitational sequestration. The abrupt density jump refracted the galactic disk’s pale backlight, blinding the station’s forward quartz arrays with sudden optical glare. The Ophiuchus stream vanished from her displays, replaced by raw static that washed out the delicate orbital paths.

A less experienced epigrapher might have triggered the automated safety factor interlocks, a protocol that would have severed the high-energy sensor links and yielded to the dark. If the core slipped into the churning noise of the inner halo, it would take another three human generations for its orbital ellipse to climb back above the disk.
